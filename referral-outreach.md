# Referral Outreach — Who to Approach and What to Say

*Companion to `cemetery-outreach-email.md`, which covers permission and compliance.
This file is about **referrals**: people who talk to families who need us.*

---

## Why this is worth more than ads

Nobody wakes up and searches "headstone cleaning." They think about it when something
reminds them — a funeral, a visit, an upcoming anniversary. Referral partners are standing
exactly at those moments, which is why one good relationship here can outperform a month of ad spend.

**One rule before you start:** don't offer cash for referrals. It's unnecessary, it puts the
partner in an awkward position (especially funeral directors, who are held to professional
standards around steering families), and a service that pays for referrals looks worse than
one that earns them. Lead with usefulness. If a partner later asks about a commission, deal
with it then.

---

## Who to approach, in priority order

### 1. Cemetery sextons and grounds staff — **start here**
The single most underrated group. They're on the grounds every day, they see which markers
are neglected, and families ask them "who can clean this?" constantly. Right now they have
no good answer. Give them one.

They're also easy to reach — you already contacted all six offices about permissions, so
there's a warm thread to reply to.

**Ask for:** permission to leave a small stack of cards in the office.

### 2. Monument companies — *including your competitors*
Counterintuitive but real. Beesley, Precious Stones, and Heritage all treat cleaning as a
sideline to selling and installing monuments. A single $65 cleaning across town is a job
they may genuinely not want — and turning it down empty-handed makes them look unhelpful.
Being the person they hand it to costs them nothing and earns you work no one else wanted.

**Ask for:** "If a cleaning-only job isn't worth your truck, send it to us."

### 3. Funeral homes
Highest volume, longest sales cycle. A director won't refer strangers, so the first
conversation is about *who you are*, not what you charge. Expect to visit more than once.

**Ask for:** to be mentioned in aftercare conversations, or included in aftercare materials.

### 4. Genealogy and family-history groups
Utah County is unusually strong here. People researching ancestors regularly find neglected
family graves several states from where they live — which is precisely the service.
Local family-history centres, genealogy societies, and Find a Grave volunteers.

**Ask for:** a mention in a newsletter, or to speak for ten minutes at a meeting.

### 5. Estate attorneys and senior-living communities
Lower volume, but they deal with exactly the families who have out-of-state relatives and
memorials nobody is visiting.

## Email — Funeral homes

> **Subject:** Local headstone cleaning — a resource for your families
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I'm Jacob Bingham. My friend Isaac Otto and I run Sacred Stone, a headstone cleaning
> service here in Utah County. I'm not writing to sell you anything — I'd just like you to
> know we exist, in case it's ever useful.
>
> Families sometimes ask months or years later what can be done about a marker that's gone
> dark with hard water or lichen. It's not really anyone's job to answer that, and we started
> Sacred Stone because we noticed the same thing at our own grandparents' graves.
>
> What we do: gentle, stone-safe cleaning — no bleach, no acid, no pressure washing — as a
> one-time visit or on a recurring schedule. Every visit ends with before-and-after photos
> emailed to the family, which matters most for the ones who live out of state and can't
> check for themselves. Our prices are published on our site, and families don't pay until
> the work is done and they've seen the photos.
>
> If it would ever be helpful to have a name to give someone, we'd be glad to be that name.
> I'm happy to drop off a few cards, or to meet if you'd rather put a face to it.
>
> Either way, thank you for the work you do.
>
> Jacob Bingham
> Sacred Stone · [phone] · [website]

**Why it's written that way:** no discount, no pitch, no urgency. A funeral director's
instinct is to protect families from being sold to, so anything that smells like a sales
push gets deleted. The "we noticed it at our own grandparents' graves" line is the part
that earns a reply — it's true, and it explains why two college students are doing this.

## Email — Monument companies (including competitors)

> **Subject:** Cleaning-only jobs — happy to take them off your hands
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I'm Jacob Bingham with Sacred Stone. We're a small Utah County outfit that does one thing:
> cleaning headstones and markers. We don't sell, engrave, set, or repair monuments — and we
> have no plans to.
>
> I imagine you get calls asking just for a cleaning, and that a single marker across the
> county isn't always worth sending a crew out for. If that's ever the case, we'd be glad to
> take those. We use preservation-safe methods only — no bleach, no acid, no pressure washing
> — and we'd represent the referral respectfully.
>
> Happy to send our process and our rates so you know exactly what you'd be handing off. And
> if a cleaning customer of ours ever needs actual monument work, I'd much rather send them
> to someone local than have them search for it.
>
> Jacob Bingham
> Sacred Stone · [phone] · [website]

**Why it works:** it opens by removing the threat — *we don't do what you do* — and closes
with a genuine offer of reciprocal work. You will get referrals out of this that you'd never
get from advertising, because these firms already own the search rankings you're competing for.

## How to actually run this

- **Go in person where you can.** For funeral homes and cemetery offices, a two-minute visit
  with cards beats ten emails. You are two polite local kids — that plays far better in a
  doorway than in an inbox.
- **You need cards.** Name, phone, website, one line: *"Headstone cleaning · Utah County ·
  Photos after every visit."* A hundred cards is about $20.
- **Go after the Stage 1 weekend, not before.** Walking in with before-and-after photos on
  your phone is a fundamentally different conversation than describing a service that has no
  completed work behind it. Do the free cleanings first.
- **Track it.** Add a tab to the orders spreadsheet: who, where, date contacted, response,
  follow-up date. Most of these need a second touch, and you will not remember.
- **Ask every customer where they heard about you**, and log it. Within a couple of months
  this tells you which partners actually produce, and which are just polite.
- **Don't promise what you can't do yet.** No insurance until the policy is bound, and no
  claims about volume or years in business. If someone asks how long you've been doing this,
  say you started this year. It's disarming, and it's true.
